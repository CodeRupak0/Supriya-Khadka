Title: BORDER AMBIGUITY BETWEEN INDIA AND NEPAL OVER KALAPANI REGION

Author: — Supriya Khadka
Image: images/blog1.jpg

Content: The border dispute between Nepal and India is not a recent issue; rather, it is a centuries-long geopolitical matter that has repeatedly shaped the diplomatic relations between the two neighboring countries. Nepal and India share an open border system, which has its own separate advantages and disadvantages. On one hand, the open border has strengthened people-to-people relations, trade, employment opportunities, inter-marriages (“roti-beti” relations), and cultural exchange. On the other hand, it has also raised concerns regarding illegal trade, border encroachment allegations, human trafficking, cross-border crimes, and national security concerns. The border connecting Nepal and India extends approximately 1,751 kilometers (1,088 miles) and touches several districts of Nepal, making the relationship between the two countries socially inseparable, yet politically sensitive.

Looking back at history, Nepal during the expansionist era of the British East India Company (BEIC) entered into the Anglo-Nepal War (1814–1816) after disputes over territorial control emerged. Prior to the Sugauli Treaty, the territorial extent of Nepal was significantly larger, stretching from the Teesta River in the east to the Sutlej River in the west , covering areas that presently lie in India. The war eventually resulted in the Sugauli Treaty of 1816, signed between the Kingdom of Nepal and the British East India Company, under which Nepal lost significant territories including Kumaon, Garhwal, Sikkim, and parts of the Terai region, many of which continue to remain under present-day Indian Territory. The treaty further recognized the Kali (Mahakali) River as the western boundary of Nepal, a clause that later became the root cause of the Kalapani–Lipulekh–Limpiyadhura dispute, as both Nepal and India differ on the interpretation of the river’s true origin.

Similarly, the unofficial border blockade of 2015 allegedly imposed during the period of Nepal’s constitutional promulgation has left a separate imprint on the Nepali psyche. Although India denied officially imposing any blockade and cited security concerns at the border due to protests in the Madhesh region, the practical consequences faced by ordinary Nepali people were severe. This issue affected several sectors, particularly the import of food, fuel, medicines, cooking gas, and other essential commodities, creating shortages across the country. Since Nepal remains heavily dependent on India for trade and imports, the blockade became not only an economic challenge but also a moment that strengthened nationalist sentiment within Nepal and increased demands for self-reliance, as well as the diversification of diplomatic and trade relations.

Likewise, another issue that has continued to dominate Nepal’s political and diplomatic discussions is the dispute surrounding Limpiyadhura, Lipulekh, and Kalapani, located in the far-western region of Nepal near the tri-junction of Nepal, India, and China. This disputed territory carries importance not only between Nepal and India but also in the context of China due to its strategic location. The Lipulekh Pass serves as an important trade and pilgrimage route connecting India and the Tibet Autonomous Region of China, particularly for the Kailash Mansarovar pilgrimage, while also carrying military and geopolitical significance because of its proximity to the India-China border.

These lands remain strategically important for all three countries. In the context of India and China, bilateral agreements  have, from time to time, recognized Lipulekh as a trade and transit route, which Nepal has repeatedly objected to, arguing that any agreement concerning disputed territory should not be made without Nepal’s consent. For instance, the 2015 India-China joint statement referring to Lipulekh Pass for expanding border trade had already raised diplomatic concerns in Nepal. Likewise, from time to time, there have been published maps on the part of both Indian and Chinese authorities, where either these territories were mapped under India’s territorial claim or Nepal’s changed territorial boundary was ignored.

The issue raised sharply in 2020, when India’s Defence Minister Rajnath Singh inaugurated a road link from Dharchula (Uttarakhand) to Lipulekh Pass through video conferencing . India stated that the road was developed to facilitate easier access for pilgrims travelling to Kailash Mansarovar and to improve border connectivity. However, Nepal strongly objected, claiming that portions of the road passed through Nepali territory. This incident eventually led Nepal to officially incorporate Lipulekh, Kalapani, and Limpiyadhura into its national map on 20 May 2020, followed by the amendment of the national emblem and constitutional schedule to reflect the revised map. The map amendment was supported unanimously in Parliament, reflecting an uncommon moment of political consensus in Nepal despite domestic political divisions. However, India rejected Nepal’s revised map, terming it an “artificial enlargement of territorial claims,” and has continued its administrative and security presence in the disputed areas.

Even today, several official and unofficial sources continue to reflect these territories as part of the Uttarakhand State of India. On the ground reality, India exercises de facto administrative control over the area, including security deployment, road access, and governance through nearby villages such as Gunji, Nabi (Nabhi), and Kuti. The residents in nearby villages are largely administered under Indian systems and identify themselves as Indian nationals. This practical reality has further complicated the dispute, as sovereignty claims are not only determined by historical maps but are also influenced by effective administration and local governance.

The Economic Times, in its article published on 14 June 2020 titled “Land in Kalapani, Lipulekh belongs to India’s border villages” , claimed that the land historically and culturally belongs to people from India’s border villages. According to the report, local land records allegedly show that portions of the disputed land are registered in the names of residents of villages on the Indian side of the border. Likewise, villagers reportedly claimed ancestral cultivation and cultural ties to the land dating back generations. Such reports indicate that, in practical terms, both occupation and administration presently remain under India.

However, the issue goes beyond mere occupation or administrative presence. The real questions remain: Are the people living in these areas willing to alter their present nationality or identity if sovereignty changes? Will the Indian government voluntarily suspend its administrative activities in the disputed territory? Will Nepal and India find a mutually acceptable diplomatic mechanism to resolve this dispute, or will it continue for generations like other unresolved territorial conflicts in South Asia? More importantly, can Nepal successfully negotiate either a bilateral understanding on the use of the land or reclaim the territory entirely through diplomatic means? , and many more.

This issue is especially sensitive because neither military conflict nor intense nationalist sentiments can offer a long-term solution. Since Nepal and India share deep historical, economic, and cultural ties, the most practical way forward lies in diplomatic dialogue, careful examination of historical records and maps, and responsible political leadership. With the rise of the new government in Nepal, the issue has once again gained political momentum. Recently, Prime Minister Balendra Shah’s debated parliamentary speech on border concerns has further pressured the government to address the matter diplomatically and with urgency. The sooner both governments engage in sincere bilateral dialogue, the stronger and healthier diplomatic relations between the two nations can remain in the future.
